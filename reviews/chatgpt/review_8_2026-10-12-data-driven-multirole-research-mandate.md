## 2. Each role's contribution to discovery

**Proposed workflow — reasoning only, to be tested rather than represented as a profitable system.**

| Assigned role | Reasoning contribution | Concrete output |
|---|---|---|
| Data/backtesting engineer | What observations were actually available at the decision time, and how dependable are they? | Timestamped, aligned inputs and explicit missing-data limits |

| Options strategist | How do direction, magnitude, timing and volatility expectations translate into actual F&O exposure? | Comparison of feasible instruments and structures |

These are cooperating analytical lenses. The same evidence must not be counted repeatedly because several roles cite it. A risk or execution rejection can override an attractive prediction.

## 3. How the roles work together

1. **Observe:** assemble the usable market, contract and contextual data available at each decision time.
2. **Explain:** market, macro and corporate perspectives propose mechanisms and alternatives. Each states what observation would contradict its hypothesis.
3. **Estimate:** the statistical perspective checks the information's predictive contribution and estimates direction, magnitude, timing and uncertainty. The owner's 1-, 3- and 5-session interests can be separate forecast horizons where appropriate.
4. **Translate:** mathematics and options perspectives compare the expected net payoff of feasible trades under those estimated outcomes.
5. **Constrain:** execution, regulation and risk perspectives reject trades that do not meet practical and account constraints.
6. **Allocate:** the portfolio perspective compares surviving trades with holding cash and with existing exposures.
7. **Learn:** compare recorded forecasts and decisions with subsequent outcomes. Revise through documented research cycles; do not change a live rule merely to explain its last loss.

The decision target is account-relevant net return under risk constraints. A more precise stock-price forecast, a higher win rate or a convincing explanation is useful only to the extent that it improves that target.

For derivatives, direction alone is insufficient: the trade's outcome also depends on size and timing of the move, option prices and changes in volatility. A correct directional prediction can still generate a losing trade after costs. These relationships must be evaluated through the actual payoff and trade lifecycle.
